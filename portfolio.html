About

Halfway through a stalled meeting about a monitoring dashboard, I walked to the whiteboard. A stakeholder said “product.” A developer said “application.” A manager said “team.” Three words, one shape — and the project had been stuck for weeks over it. That’s the work: not building the dashboard, noticing the gap.

Delivery markets, futures, supply chains, forecasting, replenishment — most people treat these as separate worlds. They’re one machine, more tightly coupled than almost anyone inside it realizes. I worked its length: seed in the dirt, grain harvested and traded, cargo exported, a product on a supermarket shelf. The names change at every step; the structure underneath doesn’t. Markets aren’t a side interest — I passed CFA Level I in 2016 and still trade futures and options on a macro-informed portfolio.

My job is to stabilize meaning under pressure: hold language steady long enough to act on, and compress it precisely enough that someone else can implement it — whether that someone is a farmer, an engineer, an AI, or an hourly worker checking grain for mycotoxins.

How I work

Method

The design work is the work

A cross-team project sat stalled for months and shipped two weeks after we did the design work — the shared language, the mockups, the definition of done. It didn’t unstick because someone wrote better code.

I navigate; the engineer drives

Paired work is rally racing: one navigator reading the course, one driver executing. I read what’s coming and call it precisely, on time — whether the driver is an engineer or an AI.

Make it fail early

Put the assumption on the cheapest surface that gets a real reaction — a sentence, a Paint mockup, an Include/Exclude table. Cheap surfaces invite correction before the cost of being wrong compounds.

Stories from the field

Notes

The Boot Pit The worst job I’ve ever been asked to do — and why I still smile about it. GrainSupply Chain

As a grain-merchant intern during a 100-year Mississippi flood, the other intern and I were sent to clean out a river-terminal boot pit — 25 feet down, three semi-loads of corn fermenting in old river water and July heat. A vac kept plugging, so it came down to a five-gallon bucket on a nylon rope: I filled it below, he hauled it up. I smelled like rotting corn for days and had to buy a new wallet.

But misery pulls people together. I still remember him singing Kenny Chesney (very well) as he pulled up the bucket, both of us laughing. The lesson stuck: the unglamorous work is where teams are actually forged.

The Mycotoxin Email I left no stone unturned. That was exactly the problem. GrainSupply Chain

New on the job, I wrote a meticulous, paragraph-long email telling hourly elevator workers how to check grain for mycotoxins. The replies came back annoyed. The ops manager was honest: “I only read the first two sentences. It was so long I didn’t have the attention span — and I bet most of them didn’t either. Send it again: three to five bullet points, one or two sentences each.”

I did. Problem solved. Brevity is respect — fitness for the audience beats completeness of information. I’ve repeated that lesson on three continents since.

The Batch-Job Dashboard Stalled for months. Shipped in two weeks. The difference wasn’t code. Solution ArchitectureSupply Chain

Two product owners wanted a dashboard to monitor batch-job health across every system, country, and supply-chain team. The inherited project had gone nowhere for months — a pie-in-the-sky requirements list, no shared language, both sides blaming each other.

What unstuck it wasn’t engineering. I ran a 45-minute alignment call, built UI mockups people could react to, wrote a definition of done in shared language, then paired with an engineer (me navigating) to ship the MVP in two weeks. It became a cross-product monitoring platform with auto-ticketing and outlier detection — recognized at every leadership level, not for the code, but for the outcome.

“Sold!” The most credible person in the room interrupted my demo with one word. Commodity TradingSolution Architecture

I presented a Flask quoting app at the company’s annual grain-merchant meeting: merchant requests a quote, trader approves, price is the net premium of the options combo. Halfway through, the merchant with the largest book of business in the company raised his hand and shouted “Sold!”

That was the design landing in real time — not a sign-off, but the most credible person in the room already seeing himself quoting a collar to a farmer with it. His body decided before his calendar did.
